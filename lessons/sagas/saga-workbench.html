Systems lesson 26 · Sagas

Saga Workbench

Booking a trip touches several services that share no transaction: the flight, the hotel, the car, the card. A saga runs each step on its own and, when a later one fails, runs the undo of every earlier step. This lesson builds one: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

01

The machine

Folders ride past five counters. Each counter books its part and hangs a tag; when the card is declined or a board is full, the folder rides back on the lower track and the tags come down. Try Decline the next card, then switch to No undo and watch tags pile up for trips that never happen.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the machine: three minutes of customers, averaged over eight seeded runs.

1. A card is declined after everything is booked

Experiment 1 of 5
No saga · each counter is called in turn and nothing is undone · 20% of cards are declined · 3 minutes of customers
The flight, hotel and car are booked, then the card is declined. What happens to those three bookings?

Each counter committed its own booking in its own database. Nothing ties them together, so when payment fails nobody tells the flight, hotel and car counters. Their tags stay on the boards, holding stock no one will use, and later customers are turned away for it. A saga gives every step an undo and runs those undos, newest first, when a later step fails.

2. Undo calls that time out

Experiment 2 of 5
Saga · a quarter of undo calls time out, and half of those actually ran before the reply was lost · 40% of cards declined
Compare three policies for a timed-out undo: give up, retry, or retry with the same idempotency key. What does each leave behind?

A timeout tells you nothing: the undo may or may not have happened. Giving up leaves the trip stuck with bookings nobody will release. Retrying is the only way forward, but a retry of an undo that already ran releases someone else's room or refunds twice. The idempotency key lets the counter recognise the repeat and do nothing, so the saga can retry until it gets a definite answer.

3. Send the confirmation first

Experiment 3 of 5
Order: email → flight → hotel → car → payment, instead of email last · 20% of cards declined
Of the trips that fail after the email went out, how many customers received a confirmation?

Some steps have no undo: an email, a text message, a shipped parcel. The best the saga can do is send a second "sorry, cancelled" message. So steps that can't be compensated go after the last step that can fail (the pivot), where nothing can roll them back anymore.

4. Few seats, busy day

Experiment 4 of 5
5 seats on each board · a customer every 2.5 s · 40% of cards declined · payment checked last
Customers are turned away because the flight is full. How many of them were blocked by a seat held by a trip that was later undone?

A saga has no isolation: every step's booking is visible to everyone the moment it commits, even though the trip may still be undone. Doomed trips hold seats for several seconds, and full boards turn real customers away. Checking the step most likely to fail first (here, the card) means doomed trips never hold anything. Other fixes: pending holds that expire, or a semantic lock other trips can see.

5. Drop the orchestrator

Experiment 5 of 5
Choreography: each counter hands the folder to the next directly, and back on failure · no desk, no checklist
What changes?

Without the round trip to the desk, every step is quicker. But no one holds the whole picture: to answer "where is my trip?" you ask each counter in turn, and a folder in transit between counters shows up nowhere. Orchestration costs a hop per step and a central component; choreography spreads the saga's logic across services and makes it harder to follow and to change.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1Why can't you wrap "book flight, book hotel, charge card" in one database transaction?
A good answer coversEach service owns its own database, and often a third party owns it (an airline, a payment provider). There is no shared transaction across them, and holding locks across slow remote calls (two-phase commit) would block everyone and fail when any participant is down. A saga accepts that each step commits on its own and plans the way back instead.
Q2What makes a good compensation?
A good answer coversIt undoes the business effect, not the history: a refund, a cancellation, a release. It is idempotent (safe to run twice, usually through an idempotency key), it can be retried until it gives a definite answer, and it should not be able to fail for business reasons. If it can, the saga needs a human or a parking place for stuck trips.
Q3Where do you put a step that can't be undone, and a step that is likely to fail?
A good answer coversLikely-to-fail steps go early, so failures happen before much has to be undone. Steps that can't be compensated (emails, shipping) go after the pivot, the last step that can fail, so they only run for trips that will definitely complete.
Q4A saga has no isolation. Describe an anomaly and a way to soften it.
A good answer coversOther requests see intermediate state: a seat held by a trip that will be undone turns a real customer away, or a refund reads a balance mid-saga. Mitigations: order steps so doomed trips fail early, use pending states that others can see and skip (a semantic lock), reservations that expire, and updates that commute so order doesn't matter.
Q5When would you choose orchestration, and when choreography?
A good answer coversOrchestration when the flow has many steps or branches, when you need to see and change it in one place, and when support must answer "where is this order?". Choreography when there are few steps and teams want services to react to events without a central coordinator, accepting that the flow is spread across services and harder to trace.
Q6An undo call to the hotel times out. What do you know, and what do you do?
A good answer coversNothing: the cancel may have run and only the reply was lost. Retry with the same idempotency key until the hotel gives a definite answer; never assume either way. If retries run out, park the saga as stuck and alert someone, because its bookings are still holding stock.
