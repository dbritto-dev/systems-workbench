Systems lesson 13 · Concurrency

Concurrency Workbench

Concurrency is one worker keeping many tasks in progress by switching between them. It hides waiting, but it never adds a second pair of hands. This lesson builds that idea three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same kitchen model the figure runs, timed to the hundredth of a second.

1. Dishes that mostly simmer

Experiment 1 of 5
4 dishes, each 3 s of stirring and 70% simmering · one at a time → switch when a pan starts simmering
How much sooner is everything served?

One at a time, the cook stands beside each pan while it simmers, so most of the run is waiting. Switching lets the cook stir another pan during every simmer. The stirring work is the same; what disappears is the idle standing. This is what concurrency buys: overlapping the waits of many tasks with one worker.

2. Dishes that never simmer

Experiment 2 of 5
4 dishes, each 3 s of nonstop stirring · one at a time → round robin, 1 s each
Does taking turns help when there is no waiting at all?

With no waits to fill, there is nothing to overlap. The cook still stirs 12 s in total, plus every switch between pans, so the whole batch takes longer. Worse, one at a time serves the first dish after 3 s; taking turns keeps every dish half-done until near the end. Concurrency helps with waiting, not with work.

3. A very short turn

Experiment 3 of 5
Round robin, 4 nonstop dishes, switching costs 0.3 s · turns of 3 s → 0.3 s
What share of the cook's time goes on moving between pans?

Each turn is followed by a switch. When a turn is as short as a switch, the cook spends as long moving as stirring, so half the time is overhead and the batch takes about twice as long. Operating systems face the same trade-off: short time slices feel responsive but each context switch costs time and cache.

4. One huge stew

Experiment 4 of 5
A 12 s stew and four 1 s dishes, all nonstop, stew first in line · finish each step before moving → round robin, 1 s turns
What happens to the four small dishes?

Without turns, the stew holds the cook for 12 s and every small dish waits behind it (head-of-line blocking). With 1 s turns the cook finishes each small dish on its first visit. The stew itself is served later, and the batch takes a little longer, but the short jobs stop paying for the long one. That is why schedulers preempt.

5. More pans on the stove

Experiment 5 of 5
Keep every pan busy, 70% simmering · 4 pans → 8 pans
What happens to dishes served per minute, and to the time each dish takes?

With 4 pans the cook is already busy all the time, stirring or moving. Extra pans add nothing the cook can serve; they only queue for the cook, so each dish waits longer. Dishes in progress = dishes per minute × time per dish (Little's law): with the rate fixed, twice the pans means twice the time.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1What is the difference between concurrency and parallelism, in terms of the cook?
A good answer coversConcurrency is one cook keeping several dishes in progress by switching between them. Parallelism is several cooks stirring at the same instant. Concurrency is about structure (dealing with many things at once); parallelism is about execution (doing many things at once).
Q2When does interleaving with one worker make things faster, and when does it make them slower?
A good answer coversFaster when tasks spend time waiting (simmering, I/O, network), because the waits can overlap with other work. Slower when tasks are pure work, because nothing overlaps and every switch adds cost.
Q3A teammate halves the time slice "to make the app more responsive". What do you check first?
A good answer coversHow much a switch costs compared with the slice. If switching is close to the slice length, most of the time goes on overhead and total throughput drops. Responsiveness for short tasks improves, but only up to the point where switching dominates.
Q4Why can one long task make many short tasks slow, and what fixes it?
A good answer coversWithout preemption, the short tasks queue behind the long one (head-of-line blocking). Time slices (preemption), a separate queue or priority for short work, or splitting the long task into chunks all let short tasks through.
Q5You raise concurrency from 4 to 8 and throughput doesn't move. What does that tell you?
A good answer coversThe worker is already saturated; the bottleneck is the worker, not the number of tasks in flight. Extra concurrency only adds queueing, so latency rises (Little's law). Find the bottleneck or add workers instead.
Q6Where do you see this one-worker pattern in real systems?
A good answer coversA Node.js or Python asyncio process interleaving requests on one thread; a single database connection multiplexing queries; an OS scheduling many threads on one core; a goroutine scheduler. The same trade-offs apply: overlap waits, pay for switches, beware long tasks.
