Systems lesson 37 · Reciprocal rank fusion

Rank Fusion Workbench

Hybrid search asks several rankers the same question and has to merge their answers, but their scores can't be compared: a keyword score of 20 and a cosine similarity of 0.8 mean nothing side by side. Reciprocal rank fusion ignores the scores and adds up 1 / (k + rank) from every list. This lesson turns it into a count room: watch the votes go in, predict what changes the result, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then runPredice y ejecuta

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same fusion code as the machine: the figure's query, and an average over 40 seeded queries with 300 documents each.

1. Add the raw scores, or fuse the ranks

Experiment 1 of 5Experimento 1 de 5
Keyword scores run from about 4 to 30, meaning scores (cosine similarity) from 0.2 to 0.9 · top 20 from each · raw sum vs RRF with k = 60 · 40 queries, 10 relevant documents each
How many relevant documents does each put in the top 10?

A keyword score of 20 and a cosine of 0.8 are on different scales, so adding them is just keyword search with a little noise: the meaning ranker can move a document by less than one keyword point. RRF throws the scores away and keeps only each ranker's order, so both rankers count equally and documents that either one finds well can reach the top. Normalising the scores first (min-max, z-scores) helps, but it depends on what else came back for that query; ranks need no calibration.

2. Two good ranks or one first place

Experiment 2 of 5Experimento 2 de 5
The figure's query, top 8 from each ballot, k = 60 · D1 is 3rd for keyword and 4th for meaning · D2 is 1st for keyword and not on the meaning ballot at all
Which document finishes higher?

With k = 60 a first place is worth 1/61 and a 3rd or 4th place nearly as much (1/63, 1/64), so a document both rankers like collects two nearly full votes, while a document only one ranker likes collects one. That is the point of fusion: agreement between rankers that look at a document differently is strong evidence. D2 ("Reset your login password") is a typical keyword false positive: it shares the words but not the question.

3. Turning k

Experiment 3 of 5Experimento 3 de 5
The same two ballots · k = 1, 60, 1000 · and the average over 40 queries
What happens to the top five at k = 1?

k sets how much the top of each list dominates. A first place is worth (k + 10) / (k + 1) times a 10th place: 5.5× at k = 1, 1.15× at k = 60, 1.01× at k = 1000. Small k rewards whatever any one ranker put first; large k mostly counts how many lists a document is on. 60, from the original paper, sits between and holds up well across collections, which is why it is the default nearly everywhere.

4. A third judge who knows nothing about the query

Experiment 4 of 5Experimento 4 de 5
Add a clicks ranker (overall popularity, unrelated to this query) · equal weight, then weight 0.3 · 40 queries, top 20 from each
With the clicks judge at full weight, what happens?

RRF gives every list the same say, so a list that ranks by something else entirely adds votes for the wrong documents, and a popular document that one other ranker also likes (like D2 here) jumps to first place. Weighted RRF (w / (k + rank)) lets you keep a signal you want as a tie-breaker without letting it outvote the rankers that understand the query. Tune the weights on queries whose right answers you know.

5. How deep each list goes

Experiment 5 of 5Experimento 5 de 5
Each ranker returns its top 10 vs its top 100 before fusing · k = 60 · 40 queries, 10 relevant documents each
How many relevant documents reach the fused top 10?

Below the cut a document gets nothing, however well it was ranked by the other list. With deeper lists, a document both rankers put around 15th collects two votes of about 1/75, more than any single first place (1/61). One deep vote on its own is still small (a 40th place is worth 1/100), so deeper lists rescue agreement, not stragglers. The cost is fetching and scoring more candidates from each index; 50 to 100 per list is common before fusing, and a reranker then sorts the top 20 or so.

03

Explain it backExplícalo

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.Responde en voz alta o por escrito antes de abrir cada una. Funcionan bien en pareja: una persona explica y la otra pregunta "¿por qué?" dos veces.

Q1P1Why can't you add a BM25 score and a cosine similarity and sort by the sum?
A good answer coversUna buena respuesta cubreThey're on different scales with different shapes: BM25 is unbounded and depends on the query's length and the collection's word statistics, while cosine similarities from an embedding model crowd into a narrow band. The sum is decided by whichever has the bigger spread, so it is mostly one ranker. Normalising (min-max, z-scores) depends on what else came back for that query and is thrown off by outliers. RRF uses only the order, so there is nothing to calibrate.
Q2P2Write the RRF formula and score a document ranked 2nd by one list and 5th by another, with k = 60.
A good answer coversUna buena respuesta cubrescore(d) = Σ over lists of 1 / (k + rank of d in that list); a list that doesn't contain d adds nothing. Here 1/62 + 1/65 ≈ 0.01613 + 0.01538 = 0.03151, almost twice a single first place (1/61 ≈ 0.01639).
Q3P3What does k do, and why is 60 the usual value?
A good answer coversUna buena respuesta cubreIt controls how top-heavy the fusion is: a first place is worth (k + 10)/(k + 1) times a 10th. Small k lets one ranker's first place win; large k mostly counts how many lists include a document. Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher found 60 worked well across their collections, and it is robust enough that most systems never tune it.
Q4P4When would you weight one ranker less, and how?
A good answer coversUna buena respuesta cubreWhen it is weaker, noisier or measures something other than relevance to the query: popularity, recency, a small fine-tuned model you don't trust yet, or keyword search on queries that are mostly natural language. Weighted RRF uses w / (k + rank). Pick the weights on a set of queries whose right answers you know, not by feel.
Q5P5What does RRF throw away, and when might a score-based fusion be better?
A good answer coversUna buena respuesta cubreThe size of the gaps: a document that wins by a mile and one that wins by a hair look the same. If your scores are calibrated (comparable probabilities) or you have labelled queries to tune a normalised weighted sum, a score-based combination can edge out RRF. RRF is the strong default because it needs no calibration and no training data.
Q6P6How deep should each list be before fusing, and what does depth cost?
A good answer coversUna buena respuesta cubreDeep enough that documents both rankers like but neither ranks near the top can still meet: with k = 60, two votes in the teens outscore one first place. Common practice is the top 50 to 100 from each index, fused, then a reranker or a cutoff of 20 or so. Deeper lists cost latency and compute on every query, and past a point add only single deep votes that never reach the top.
