Systems lesson 03 · Load balancing

Load Balancer Workbench

A load balancer decides which server gets each request. That one decision, made thousands of times a second, sets how long users wait. This lesson builds the idea three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the figure, averaged over six runs of ten minutes each.

1. One more counter

Experiment 1 of 5
Counters 3 → 4 at 0.9 parcels/s · a third more capacity, same traffic
What happens to the slowest 5% of waits (p95)?

At 90% busy, any small burst lands on a long queue and waits behind it; at 68% busy, queues rarely form at all. Waiting grows roughly like busy ÷ (1 − busy), so the last few points of utilisation cost far more than the first. That is why teams keep headroom instead of running servers near 100%.

2. One slow counter

Experiment 2 of 5
Counter 1 works at a third of the speed · 4 counters, round robin
With round robin, what happens to p95?

Round robin hands every counter the same share, a quarter of the parcels, whatever it can handle. The slow counter can only finish about half of its share, so its queue grows without limit, and every parcel sent to it waits longer than the last. Least connections looks at how many parcels each counter still has and routes around the slow one.

3. Uneven parcels

Experiment 3 of 5
1 parcel in 10 takes 11× longer than the rest · same total work
Switching from round robin to least connections changes p95 by…

Round robin doesn't know a counter is stuck on a big parcel, so it keeps sending short parcels into that lane, where they wait for 16 seconds. Least connections sends them to a counter that is free. Random is worse than both: it can pile several parcels on one counter by chance.

4. A counter crashes

Experiment 4 of 5
Counter 2 dies · the balancer only finds out at its next health check
With a health check every 30 s, how many parcels fail?

Between the crash and the next check, the balancer still believes the counter is up and keeps sending it a quarter of the traffic. Faster checks shrink that window, but they cannot save the parcels that were already inside the counter when it died: that floor stays the same at any interval.

5. Sticky customers

Experiment 5 of 5
Two customers send 40% of the parcels · sticky sessions pin each customer to one counter
Twenty customers in total. What happens to the counters?

Sticky sessions decide by customer, not by load. Here both big customers hash to the same counter, so it gets half the traffic and more work than it can do, while the others sit at under half busy. The balancer's algorithm never gets a say for those parcels.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1At 90% busy, adding a third more capacity cut p95 by more than half. Why isn't the gain proportional?
A good answer coversWaiting time grows roughly like utilisation ÷ (1 − utilisation): at 0.9 that is 9, at 0.68 about 2. Near full, every burst queues behind the last one. Leave headroom (many teams aim for 60–70% at peak) and scale before you reach the steep part of the curve.
Q2Round robin is fine when servers are identical. When does it hurt, and why does least connections help?
A good answer coversIt hurts when servers differ in speed (an old instance, a noisy neighbour, a server doing GC) or requests differ in cost. Round robin ignores both and keeps feeding a backed-up server. Least connections uses outstanding requests as a cheap signal of how busy each server really is.
Q3Least connections has a trap: a broken server that returns errors instantly has almost no outstanding requests. What happens?
A good answer coversIt looks like the least busy server, so the balancer sends it more and more traffic, and most requests fail (a "black hole"). Health checks, outlier detection that ejects servers with high error rates, and counting errors as failures all guard against it.
Q4Someone proposes health checks every 100 ms on a 200-server fleet. What are the trade-offs?
A good answer coversFaster detection, but checks become real load on every server, and one slow response can mark a healthy server down (flapping). Real setups combine an interval with thresholds (down after 3 failures, up after 2 passes) and passive checks that watch real traffic.
Q5Why use sticky sessions at all, and how would you avoid the hot counter from experiment 5?
A good answer coversThey keep a user on the server that holds their session or warm cache. To avoid hot spots, move session state to a shared store (Redis, a database, a signed cookie) so any server can answer, or use consistent hashing with bounded load so a full server spills to the next one.
Q6Parcels inside a crashed counter fail no matter how fast the checks are. How do real systems protect those requests?
A good answer coversRetry idempotent requests on another server (at the balancer or the client, with a budget so retries can't multiply load), drain servers before planned restarts so they finish in-flight work, and set timeouts so a request doesn't hang on a dead connection.
