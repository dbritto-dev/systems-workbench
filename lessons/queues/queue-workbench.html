Systems lesson 33 · Queues

Queue Workbench

A queue is a line: the first one in is the first one out. Put one between the code that creates work and the code that does it, and it soaks up bursts, survives crashed workers and keeps arrival order, as long as the work keeps up on average. This lesson runs a parcel office as a work queue: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

01

The machine

Parcels arrive at the dock on the left and roll along the lane to the front on the right. Clerks take the parcel at the front, work it and stamp it into the cart. When a clerk collapses before stamping, or a parcel can't be processed, the parcel waits on the hold rack until its timer runs out, then goes back to the front of the lane. After 3 tries it goes into the cage. Try the arrival rate, the bad parcels, the lane limit and the per-customer order.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the machine, averaged over eight seeded runs.

1. Slightly more than the clerks can do

Experiment 1 of 5
Arrivals 8/s → 10/s · three clerks can do 9 a second between them · run for 5 minutes
After 5 minutes at 10 a second, what does the queue look like?

Every second one more parcel arrives than leaves, so the line grows by about one a second for as long as it lasts. Little's law ties the numbers together: time waiting = queue length ÷ throughput, so 300 ÷ 9 ≈ 33 s. Just below capacity the line stays short. A queue smooths out bursts; it cannot fix an average arrival rate above the service rate. Only more clerks, cheaper work or fewer arrivals can.

2. 200 parcels at once

Experiment 2 of 5
A quiet 5/s stream that the clerks (9/s) easily keep up with · 200 parcels dropped in at once · a queue vs nowhere to wait (turned away if every clerk is busy)
What happens to the 200 parcels?

The spare capacity (9 − 5 = 4 a second) drains the burst in under a minute, so the queue turns a spike nobody could serve into a short delay. Without anywhere to wait, everything that arrives while the clerks are busy is refused. This is the main reason queues sit between services: producers and consumers no longer have to run at the same speed at the same moment.

3. A clerk collapses before stamping

Experiment 3 of 5
1 try in 10 ends with the clerk collapsing: half during the work, half after it but before the stamp · stamp after the work vs stamp on pickup vs an idempotent handler
With the stamp after the work, what happens to the parcels whose clerk collapsed?

Nobody stamped them, so when the hold timer (the visibility timeout) runs out they reappear and another clerk works them. Nothing is lost, but a parcel whose work had finished gets done twice: at-least-once delivery. Stamping on pickup flips it: no repeats, but a clerk who collapses mid-work loses the parcel for good (at-most-once). The usual answer is to stamp after the work and make the handler idempotent: it remembers parcel ids, so a repeat does no harm.

4. One parcel in 50 can never be processed

Experiment 4 of 5
4 clerks, 1.5 s per parcel, 2 arriving a second · a bad parcel fails every time · retried forever vs moved to the dead-letter cage after 3 tries · 5 minutes
With retry forever, what happens by the 5-minute mark?

A bad parcel never leaves: every few seconds it reappears, takes a clerk, fails and goes back on the rack. New bad parcels keep arriving, so the share of clerk time spent on them grows until the clerks fall behind on the good ones too. A limit on tries plus a dead-letter queue takes them out of circulation after a few attempts, so the rest flows, and leaves them in one place for a person to inspect, fix and send back.

5. One slow parcel at the front

Experiment 5 of 5
Parcels must be handled in order · one parcel for customer A takes 30 s instead of 0.4 s · one order for everything vs one order per customer (4 customers)
How long do customers B, C and D wait?

Keeping one global order means only one parcel can be out at a time, so the slow one blocks everything behind it: head-of-line blocking. Most systems only need order within one customer, account or key. Keep a separate order per key (message groups, partitions) and a free clerk can reach past A's blocked parcels to B, C and D. A still waits behind its own slow parcel, which is the order it asked for.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1As a data structure, what does a queue promise, and how do you make adding and removing O(1)?
A good answer coversFirst in, first out: add at the tail, remove from the head, in arrival order. A linked list with head and tail pointers, or a ring buffer (a fixed array with head and tail indices that wrap around), makes both O(1). Removing from the front of a plain array (array.shift()) moves every other element, so it is O(n).
Q2Your queue gets longer every afternoon and drains overnight. What is happening, and what are your options?
A good answer coversIn the afternoon work arrives faster than it is processed, and Little's law says the wait grows with the length (wait = length ÷ throughput). A bigger queue only delays the problem. The options: add consumers, make each job cheaper, or reduce what arrives (shed load, rate-limit, or bound the queue and push back on producers).
Q3Why should a queue consumer be idempotent, and when should it acknowledge a message?
A good answer coversAcknowledge after the work is done, or a crash mid-work loses the message (at-most-once). But then a crash after the work and before the ack makes the message come back, and it is processed again (at-least-once). So handlers must tolerate repeats: dedupe by message id, use an idempotency key, or make the operation naturally idempotent (set a value rather than add to it).
Q4What is a visibility timeout, and what goes wrong if it is shorter than your processing time?
A good answer coversWhile a consumer holds a message it is hidden from the others; if it isn't acknowledged before the timeout, it becomes visible again for someone else. If processing takes longer than the timeout, a second consumer picks up a message the first is still working on, so you get duplicates with no crash at all. Set it above your worst-case processing time, or extend it (a heartbeat) while you work.
Q5What is a dead-letter queue for, and what should you do with what lands in it?
A good answer coversIt catches messages that failed a set number of times (max receive count), so one poison message can't keep consuming workers or block the line. Alert on it, inspect the messages, fix the bug or the bad data, then redrive them to the main queue. A dead-letter queue nobody watches is just a slower way to lose data.
Q6Strict first-in-first-out for everything, or order per key: what do you gain and lose?
A good answer coversOne global order means one message in flight at a time, so throughput is one worker's and any slow message blocks all the others (head-of-line blocking). Ordering per key (SQS FIFO message groups, Kafka partitions) keeps order where it matters, within an account or an order, and lets different keys run in parallel. A hot or stuck key still blocks its own line. If you don't need order at all, plain parallel consumers give the most throughput.
