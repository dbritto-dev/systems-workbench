Systems lesson 20 · Logs and offsets

Kafka Log Workbench

A log keeps records in the order they arrive and doesn't delete them when they are read; each consumer group keeps a bookmark instead. This lesson builds that idea three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the machine, averaged over six seeded runs.

1. Order per customer

Experiment 1 of 5
Records with no key (any track) vs keyed by customer · 3 tracks, 3 consumers, 4 records/s
Each customer's records are created in order: created, paid, shipped. With no key, how often is a customer's record read before an earlier one of the same customer?

A log is ordered per track, not across tracks. Without a key, one customer's records land on different tracks, and each track is read by its own consumer at its own pace, so a later record can be read first. Keying by customer sends all of that customer's records to one track, where one consumer reads them in order.

2. A consumer crashes

Experiment 2 of 5
Consumer 1 crashes in the middle of a batch every 10 s · commit after processing vs before
Committing after processing, what happens to the records it had processed but not yet committed when it crashed?

After a restart a consumer continues from the committed offset, the bookmark. Commit after processing and the records processed since the last commit are read a second time (at least once), so the handler must be idempotent. Commit before processing and the records that were committed but never processed are skipped for good (at most once).

3. More consumers than tracks

Experiment 3 of 5
3 tracks · producer at 8 records/s · consumers in the group 3 → 6
Three consumers can't keep up. With six, how many records are read per minute?

Within a group each track is read by exactly one consumer, which is what keeps it in order. Three tracks means at most three consumers doing work; the other three stand by. To read faster you need more tracks (partitions), and that is much easier to plan up front than to change later.

4. A hot customer

Experiment 4 of 5
70% of records are for customer A · keyed by customer · 3 tracks, 3 consumers, 4 records/s
After a minute, where is the lag?

Every record for A goes to one track, and one consumer reads it at about 2 records/s while A alone sends 2.8. The other two tracks sit nearly empty. More tracks don't help a single key; if the order only matters per order or per device, key by that instead so the load spreads.

5. Rebuild from the log

Experiment 5 of 5
After 90 s the customer totals are wiped · every bookmark is rewound to the oldest record and read again · keep all records vs keep 20 s
Keeping every record, how many of the 6 customer totals come back right?

Reading a log doesn't remove anything; consumers only move a bookmark. Rewind the bookmark and the same records are read again, so a broken read model can be rebuilt from scratch. That only works for what the log still keeps: with 20 s of retention most of the history is already gone, and no total comes back right.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1Why does the producer pick a track by key, and what exactly does that guarantee?
A good answer coversThe same key always goes to the same track, and a track is read in order by one consumer, so all records for one key are processed in the order they were written. There is no ordering guarantee between different keys or different tracks.
Q2Commit after processing or before? Which guarantee does each give, and what does the handler need?
A good answer coversAfter processing gives at-least-once: after a crash some records are read again, so the handler must be idempotent (for example, dedupe by record id or use upserts). Before processing gives at-most-once: nothing is repeated, but records committed and not yet processed are lost.
Q3A topic has 3 partitions and you scale the consumer group to 10 to catch up. What happens, and what should you change?
A good answer coversOnly 3 consumers get a partition; 7 sit idle and throughput doesn't change. Add partitions (and plan for it early, since it changes which partition a key maps to), or make each consumer faster.
Q4One customer produces most of the traffic. What do you see, and what can you do?
A good answer coversLag builds on that customer's partition while the others are idle. More partitions don't help one key. Key by something finer when order only matters per order or per device, or split that customer's work after the log.
Q5How is a log different from a queue where reading deletes the message?
A good answer coversA log keeps records until retention removes them, whether or not anyone read them. Each consumer group has its own bookmark, so many groups can read the same records independently, and any group can rewind and replay.
Q6A consumer restarts. How does it know where to continue, and why do offsets have to keep increasing?
A good answer coversThe group stores a committed offset per partition (in Gossip Glomers this is `commit_offsets` and `list_committed_offsets`), and the consumer resumes there. Offsets only grow, so "committed offset 120" means everything below 120 is done; if offsets could repeat or go backwards, a bookmark would mean nothing.
