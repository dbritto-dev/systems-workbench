Systems lesson 08 · Canary deploys

Canary Deploy Workbench

A canary deploy sends a small share of real traffic to the new version, watches it, and either rolls it back or promotes it. This lesson builds that idea three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what fools it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the figure, averaged over many seeded runs: 1.2 orders per second, 1 in 1,000 orders fail on either version by bad luck, and the rollback arm checks every 3 minutes for at least 3 failures above 0.4%.

1. How many people does a bad build hurt?

Experiment 1 of 5
Bad build (1 in 5 orders fail) · canary 25% → 1% · automatic rollback on
At 25%, the rollback arm catches the bad build after about 11 failed orders. At 1%, how many orders fail before it is caught?

The rollback arm needs evidence: at least 3 failures before it acts. At 25% those arrive in seconds, but the check only runs every 3 minutes, so more orders fail while it waits. At 1% the canary pays roughly the evidence price and little more, but collecting it takes about 25 minutes. A small canary limits damage per minute, not the minimum damage needed to notice.

2. A rare bug

Experiment 2 of 5
1 order in 200 fails on the new build · canary 10% → 1%
At 10% the arm needs about 2 hours to be sure. At 1%, how long?

The arm needs the same evidence either way, three failures, and at 1 in 200 that means hundreds of canary orders. A 1% canary sees a tenth of the orders, so it takes several times longer. If you promote after a 30-minute bake, this bug ships at both sizes: rare bugs need more traffic or longer bakes, not luck.

3. A twitchy rollback

Experiment 3 of 5
Good build at 10% for 2 hours · threshold normal → tight (any failure above 0.15%)
The new build is fine. Both tracks fail 1 order in 1,000 by bad luck. How often does the tight rule roll it back anyway?

Healthy systems fail a little all the time. With one failure as enough evidence, a single unlucky order early in the canary looks like a regression. False rollbacks are not free: people stop trusting the arm and start overriding it. A good rule asks for several failures and compares against the old version's normal noise.

4. Who sees what

Experiment 4 of 5
Good build at 25% for 10 minutes · 100 users, each ordering about 7 times
With random routing, how many users see both the old and the new version?

Random routing decides per request, so a user with 7 orders flips between versions several times: a button moves, a cart format changes, a session written by one version is read by the other. Sticky routing decides per user, so each person stays on one version and the canary share means a share of people.

5. The bug that doesn't fail

Experiment 5 of 5
New build charges the wrong price on 3 orders in 10 · canary 10% · promoted after a 30-minute bake
The rollback arm only watches failures. After an hour, how many wrong-price orders went out?

Nothing failed, so the error gauge stayed flat and the canary looked perfect. After promotion every order went through the new build. Canaries only catch what you measure: add business checks (prices match the catalogue, orders per minute, payment success) to the analysis and the same build is rolled back within minutes.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1Why ship to 1% first instead of everyone, if the 1% canary still lets some orders fail?
A good answer coversIt limits how many people are hurt per minute while you collect evidence, and it limits who is hurt if you miss the signal. You still pay a minimum number of failures to notice anything; a canary turns a full outage into that small, bounded cost.
Q2A teammate says "our canary ran for 30 minutes at 1% with zero errors, ship it". What would you ask?
A good answer coversHow many requests actually went through the canary, and how rare a bug could that sample have caught? At 1% of modest traffic, 30 minutes may be a few hundred requests, which cannot tell a 1-in-200 bug from noise. Ask for a minimum sample size, not just a time.
Q3Your automatic rollback fires on every second deploy, but the builds turn out fine. What's going on, and what would you change?
A good answer coversThe rule treats normal background noise as a regression: too few failures required, or a threshold below the old version's own error rate. Require several failures, compare against the baseline running at the same time, and look at a window long enough to average out bad luck.
Q4When would you route the canary by user instead of by request?
A good answer coversWhenever the experience must be consistent for a person: UI changes, multi-step flows, sessions or caches written by one version and read by another, or when you want to say "5% of users" rather than "5% of requests". Request-level routing is fine for stateless backend changes.
Q5Name two things a canary analysis should watch besides HTTP 5xx errors.
A good answer coversBusiness and correctness signals: orders or sign-ups per minute, payment success, values that must match (prices against the catalogue), latency percentiles, and resource use. Silent bugs only show up in what you choose to measure.
Q6The canary is rolled back automatically. What has happened to the users who already got an error, and what does that mean for database changes?
A good answer coversRollback only stops new damage; failed orders stay failed and data the new version wrote stays written. That's why schema changes are split (expand, deploy, then contract) so either version can run against the same data, and why rollback has to be safe to do at any time.
