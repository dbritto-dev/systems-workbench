Pub/Sub Workbench

Publish/subscribe lets one part of a system announce something without knowing who listens. This lesson builds that idea three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from two simulated minutes of the same machine (100 messages), averaged over six runs.

1. More subscribers

Experiment 1 of 5
Subscribers 2 → 5 · three more stations now want every message
The press now feeds five stations instead of two. How long does it spend on each message?

The press only drops the capsule into the hub. Firing a copy down every tube is the broker's job, and all copies travel at once, and working through each tray is each station's job. That is the decoupling pub/sub buys: adding a subscriber costs the publisher nothing. If the press called each station itself and waited for an answer, every new station would add its whole processing time to every message.

2. A slow subscriber

Experiment 2 of 5
Station C takes 2 s per card · the press still prints one every 1.2 s
After two minutes, what has happened?

Each subscriber has its own queue, so a slow one only hurts itself. But a queue that fills faster than it empties grows without limit: C takes 2.35 s per card while cards arrive every 1.2 s, so it falls about 25 cards behind every minute. Queue depth (consumer lag) is the number to alert on.

3. A subscriber goes away

Experiment 3 of 5
Station C is offline for 20 s · its subscription is not durable
The cards printed while C was away…

A non-durable subscription exists only while its subscriber is connected; the broker has nowhere to put C's copies, so they are dropped. A durable subscription keeps the tray in place: the cards wait, and C works through the backlog when it returns, faster than new ones arrive.

4. Crash before the ack

Experiment 4 of 5
Stations crash 1 time in 10 after doing the work, before stamping the card
Over 100 messages, how many times does station A's handler run?

The broker only knows a card is done when it sees the stamp (the ack). A crash after the work but before the ack looks exactly like a crash before the work, so the broker hands the card out again. That is at-least-once delivery: nothing is lost, but some messages arrive twice. An idempotent handler, one that checks whether it has already applied a message, turns those duplicates into no-ops.

5. One queue, two workers

Experiment 5 of 5
A and B read from one shared queue instead of each having a subscription
Of 100 messages, how many does each of A and B get?

Two subscriptions mean two copies: each subscriber gets everything (fan-out). Two workers on one queue are competing consumers: each card goes to exactly one of them, so they share the work. Same broker, different shape: pub/sub for "everyone who cares should know", a work queue for "someone should do this".

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1The press never waits for the stations. What does it give up in exchange?
A good answer coversIt no longer knows whether, when or how a subscriber handled the message: no return value and no error comes back to it. Failures show up downstream, so you need to watch consumer lag and dead letters, and accept that the system is only eventually consistent.
Q2Station C's tray keeps growing. What would you look at, and what are three ways out?
A good answer coversLook at queue depth or consumer lag over time and C's processing time per message. Ways out: add competing consumers on C's queue, make each message cheaper (batching, fewer round trips), or bound the queue with a policy (drop, dead-letter, or slow the publisher down with backpressure).
Q3When would you choose a non-durable subscription on purpose?
A good answer coversWhen old messages are worthless to a subscriber that was away: live positions, presence, prices, a dashboard that refreshes anyway. It is cheaper and can't build up a backlog. Anything that must not be missed, like orders or payments, needs a durable subscription.
Q4Your payment handler reads from an at-least-once queue. How do you make sure a customer is charged once?
A good answer coversMake the handler idempotent: give every message an id, record processed ids (a unique constraint in the same transaction as the charge), and skip ids already seen. Pass an idempotency key to the payment provider too, so a retry on their side can't double-charge.
Q5Why is exactly-once delivery so hard, and what do teams do instead?
A good answer coversBecause the ack can be lost after the work is done, and the broker can't tell that apart from a crash before the work. So it must either redeliver (risking duplicates) or not (risking loss). Teams pick at-least-once delivery plus idempotent handlers, which gives exactly-once effects.
Q6Give a real example of pub/sub and one of a work queue from a system you know.
A good answer coversPub/sub: "order placed" goes to email, analytics and inventory, each of which needs every order. Work queue: thumbnails to generate or emails to send, where any one worker can do each job and adding workers adds throughput.
