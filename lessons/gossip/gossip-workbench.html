Systems lesson 19 · Gossip protocols

Gossip Workbench

Gossip spreads information the way a rumour moves through a village: every round, whoever knows tells a few random others. Nobody coordinates, nothing is retried, and yet everyone hears it fast. This lesson builds that idea three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the machine, averaged over 12 to 60 seeded runs.

1. Ten times the village

Experiment 1 of 5
40 houses → 400 houses · push, each house tells 2 random houses per round
How many rounds until every house has heard the rumour?

The number of houses that know roughly triples every round (each tells two more), so covering ten times the houses only takes the few extra rounds it needs to triple a couple more times. Spreading time grows with the logarithm of the size: that is why gossip scales to thousands of nodes.

2. Tell more neighbours

Experiment 2 of 5
Fanout 1 → 3 · push, 40 houses
Each house now tells 3 random houses per round instead of 1. What happens?

With a bigger fanout the rumour reaches everyone in about half the rounds, but every informed house sends three messages a round, and many of them land on houses that already know. You trade bandwidth for speed. Real systems keep fanout small (often 2 to 4) and run rounds frequently.

3. Push, pull or both

Experiment 3 of 5
Push vs pull vs push-pull · fanout 1, 40 houses
Once half the village knows, which way reaches the last houses fastest?

With push, the last few houses only hear it if some informed house happens to pick them, and most pushes land on houses that already know. With pull, every uninformed house asks someone each round, and once most houses know, almost every question gets an answer. Push-pull combines both and finishes first, at the cost of more messages, because everyone keeps asking.

4. Lose a third of the messages

Experiment 4 of 5
Message loss 0% → 30% · push-pull, fanout 2, 40 houses
Three in ten messages vanish on the way. What happens to the rumour?

Nobody depends on one particular message: every house hears the rumour from many directions, and lost messages are simply replaced by later random ones. Losing 30% only costs a round or so. This redundancy is why gossip works over unreliable networks without acknowledgements or retries.

5. How long before "dead"

Experiment 5 of 5
Failure timeout 4 → 8 → 16 rounds · heartbeats gossiped with fanout 2, slow links (3 in 10 messages arrive 1–3 rounds late)
What does a short timeout do?

A house's heartbeat needs a few rounds to travel through the gossip, more when links are slow. If the timeout is shorter than that, a perfectly healthy house looks silent and gets declared dead. A long timeout never cries wolf but takes longer to notice a real failure. The timeout has to cover the normal spreading time (which grows with the village size) plus slow links.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1Why does gossip reach a 10,000-node cluster in only a few more rounds than a 100-node cluster?
A good answer coversEach round, every node that knows tells a few others, so the number that know multiplies (roughly by fanout + 1) until most know. Multiplying gets from 100 to 10,000 in a couple more steps: spreading time grows with log N, not with N.
Q2Gossip never retries a lost message. Why does it still reach everyone?
A good answer coversEvery node hears the news from many random senders, so no single message matters; a lost one is replaced by the next random contact. Redundancy replaces acknowledgements, which is what makes gossip robust to loss, slow links and nodes coming and going.
Q3What is the difference between push, pull and push-pull, and when does each shine?
A good answer coversPush: nodes that know send it out; fast at the start, slow to reach the last few. Pull: every node asks a random peer; slow at the start, but finishes the tail quickly. Push-pull exchanges both ways and is fastest overall. Pull-style anti-entropy also repairs nodes that missed updates while they were down.
Q4What does a bigger fanout buy, and what does it cost?
A good answer coversFewer rounds to reach everyone, because the informed population grows faster, but more messages per round, many of them redundant. Most systems pick a small fanout (2 to 4) and a short round interval instead.
Q5In gossip-based failure detection, why can't the timeout be just one or two rounds?
A good answer coversA node's heartbeat reaches others only by being passed along, which takes several rounds (about log N) and longer with slow links. A timeout shorter than that declares healthy nodes dead. Systems like Cassandra use a suspicion level (phi accrual) instead of a fixed cutoff, and SWIM asks other nodes to probe a suspect before declaring it dead.
Q6Where is gossip used in practice?
A good answer coversCluster membership and failure detection (Consul/Serf with SWIM, Cassandra, Redis Cluster), spreading cluster state and metadata, anti-entropy repair between replicas, and eventually consistent data like counters and CRDTs. It suits information everyone should learn eventually, not things that need an immediate, agreed answer (that needs consensus).
