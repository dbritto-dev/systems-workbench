Systems lesson 30 · Sampling and cardinality

Cardinality Workbench

Every label on a metric multiplies how many time series it creates, and every trace you keep costs storage, so what you record has to be chosen. This lesson builds a metric and a trace sampler as one machine: watch it run, predict what an experiment will do, then explain it back.

01

The machine

One service, two records of every request. At the back, a tally card finds the pigeonhole for its exact combination of labels: one hole per time series. At the front, the request's trace goes through a sampler that keeps or drops it. Add a label, switch the route to raw paths, or change the sampler, and watch the wall, the dial and the archive.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Series counts are the expected number of distinct label combinations at 100 requests a second; sampling results average seeded runs of the same model.

1. Add a status label

Experiment 1 of 5
http_requests_total{method, route} → {method, route, status} · 2 methods × 8 routes, status has 5 values · 100 requests/s for an hour
The metric had 16 series. After adding status, how many series does it have?

A series is one combination of label values, so the count is the product of how many values each label takes: 2 × 8 × 5 = 80. Every label multiplies. Here that's fine: 80 series is a few hundred kilobytes, and every value is bounded.

2. Add user_id

Experiment 2 of 5
{method, route, status} → {method, route, status, user_id} · 10,000 active users · 100 requests/s for an hour
Compared with the 80 series before, the count after one hour is…

user_id multiplies too: every route and status a user touches becomes its own series. Not every combination happens in an hour, but a large share do, and the count keeps climbing as the day goes on. At a few kilobytes per series that is hundreds of megabytes for one metric, and queries over it slow down. Per-user detail belongs in logs and traces, which cost per event, not per combination.

3. Raw paths as the route

Experiment 3 of 5
route="/orders/{id}" → route="/orders/58213" · ids come from a catalogue of a million · {method, route, status} · 100 requests/s
How many series after one hour, and after a day?

An id in a label is an unbounded label: there is no last value, so new series keep appearing for as long as new ids are requested. This is the most common way cardinality leaks into a metrics system: raw URLs, error messages, query strings, IP addresses. Template the route before it becomes a label.

4. Head sampling at 1%

Experiment 4 of 5
Keep 1 in 100 traces, decided when the request starts · 0.1% of requests fail · one rare bug hits 1 in 10,000 · 100 requests/s, 12 hours
Each hour about 360 requests fail, 36 of them from the rare bug. How many traces of the rare bug do you keep in an hour?

The head gate decides before it knows how the request will end, so errors are kept at the same 1% as everything else: 360 errors become about 3 traces, and 36 rare failures become 0.36, so most hours have none. Head sampling is cheap and fine for the common path, but a rare failure needs far more traffic before you hold even one example. Count errors with metrics (which see every request) and keep error traces some other way.

5. Tail sampling

Experiment 5 of 5
Wait until each trace finishes, then keep every error, every slow trace (0.5%) and 1% of the rest · same traffic as before
Compared with head sampling at 1%, what does tail sampling cost?

Errors and slow requests are a small share of traffic, so keeping all of them adds little to the 1% baseline, and you keep exactly the traces you'll want to open. The price moves elsewhere: the sampler must hold every trace in memory until it is complete (all its spans have arrived) before it can decide, and all spans of a trace must reach the same sampler.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1A teammate adds user_id as a label on http_requests_total "so we can see who is affected". What happens, and what would you do instead?
A good answer coversEvery user multiplies the series: route × status × users, hundreds of thousands of series, so memory and ingestion cost jump, queries slow down, and the metrics system may start dropping data or hit limits. Keep metric labels bounded (method, route template, status class) and put user ids in logs and traces, linked from the metric by an exemplar or trace id. If you need per-customer numbers, use a small bounded label like a plan or tier.
Q2How do you estimate how many series a metric will create before you ship it?
A good answer coversMultiply the number of distinct values of every label; that's the ceiling, and real traffic hits a share of it. Remember the labels you don't write yourself: every instance or pod multiplies it again, and a histogram multiplies it by its number of buckets. If any label has no fixed list of values, the answer is "unbounded" and the label needs to change.
Q3The number of series grows every day although nothing was deployed. Where would you look?
A good answer coversFor an unbounded label: raw paths with ids, error messages, query strings, IP addresses, user agents, or pod names that change on every restart or autoscale. Find the metric with the most series and the label with the most values, then template, bucket or drop that label (for example with a relabelling rule).
Q4Head sampling and tail sampling: what does each cost, and what does each miss?
A good answer coversHead sampling decides at the start: cheap, stateless, every service follows the same decision, but it can't know a request will fail or be slow, so rare errors are mostly dropped. Tail sampling decides when the trace is complete, so it can keep every error and slow trace; it needs a buffer that holds every trace until it finishes, a wait before the decision, and all spans of a trace routed to the same sampler.
Q5With head sampling at 1%, can you still tell the error rate? Can you find the trace of one customer's failed checkout?
A good answer coversThe rate, roughly, but counts from sampled traces are noisy when errors are rare: 360 errors an hour become about 3 kept traces. Take rates from metrics, which count every request. One specific failed checkout was almost certainly dropped; to have it you need tail sampling (keep all errors), a rule that always keeps errors, or logs that carry the trace id.
Q6Metrics, logs and traces: where should high-cardinality data go, and why?
A good answer coversInto logs and traces. A metric costs per series and is kept and indexed for every combination of labels, so each label must have few values. Logs and traces cost per event, so they can carry any id: user, order, request. Link them: a trace id in the logs, exemplars on the metric pointing to example traces.
