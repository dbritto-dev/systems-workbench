Systems lesson 38 · Hybrid search

Hybrid Search Workbench

Hybrid search runs a keyword retriever and a meaning retriever on the same query and merges what they find. Each one misses a different kind of query, so the merged list catches both, if the merge is done right. This lesson runs a two-lane sorting plant over a help centre: watch it, predict what changes the results, then explain it back.

01

The machine

Each query goes down both lanes. The keyword lane (BM25) sieves articles by the query's exact words; the meaning lane (embeddings) pulls them by similarity. Their racks pour into the merge hopper, an optional reranker re-reads the top candidates, and the best ten land on the results shelf. The filled tile is the article the query wants. 02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the machine: 90 labelled queries on each of five generated help centres, averaged.

1. One lane or both

Experiment 1 of 5
Keyword lane only (BM25) vs meaning lane only (embeddings) vs both, merged with reciprocal rank fusion · 30 queries of each kind · is the wanted article in the top 10?
Which setup finds the right article for all three kinds of query?

Keyword only: exact IDs 100%, paraphrases 68%, mixed 78%. Meaning only: exact IDs 71%, paraphrases 100%, mixed 90%. Both, merged: exact IDs 97%, paraphrases 94%, mixed 98%. BM25 matches exact tokens, so a pasted error code or part number lands first, but a customer who writes "money back" never matches an article that says "refund". The embedding puts synonyms together, but an identifier or a product name means nothing to it, so it can't tell E-4072 from E-4073 or one product's article from another's. A mixed query needs both at once: the product name from the keyword lane and the meaning from the other.

2. Adding the scores as they are

Experiment 2 of 5
Merge by a weighted sum, α = 0.5, of the raw scores (BM25 runs from 0 to about 20, cosine from 0 to 1) vs the same sum after min-max scaling each list to 0..1 vs reciprocal rank fusion
With raw scores, what happens to paraphrase queries?

Raw sum: exact IDs 100%, paraphrases 75%, mixed 99%. Min-max scaled: exact IDs 100%, paraphrases 100%, mixed 96%. Reciprocal rank fusion: exact IDs 97%, paraphrases 94%, mixed 98%. The two scores live on different scales, so an unscaled sum lets whichever is numerically bigger decide, and α no longer means what it says. Scale each list first (min-max or z-scores), or merge by rank, which ignores the scores altogether. RRF needs no tuning and is the safe default; it gives both lanes the same vote, so on paraphrases the keyword lane's near-misses take a few seats that a tuned weighted sum keeps for the meaning lane.

3. Turning the balance

Experiment 3 of 5
Min-max weighted sum with α = 0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 and 1 (0 = keywords only, 1 = meaning only) · recall@10 for each kind of query
Which α is best?

α 0: exact IDs 100%, paraphrases 68%, mixed 78%. α 0.3: exact IDs 100%, paraphrases 93%, mixed 96%. α 0.5: exact IDs 100%, paraphrases 100%, mixed 96%. α 0.7: exact IDs 98%, paraphrases 100%, mixed 94%. α 1: exact IDs 71%, paraphrases 100%, mixed 90%. Best for exact IDs: α 0 to 0.5; for paraphrases: α 0.5 to 1; for mixed: α 0.3 to 0.5; over all 90: α 0.5 (99%). The ends are just the single lanes. Tune α on a labelled sample of your own queries, and retune when the mix changes.

4. How deep each lane looks

Experiment 4 of 5
Each lane hands over its top 5, 20 or 100 candidates · merged with RRF · a reranker re-reads up to 100 of them · is a wanted article in the top 10?
What does looking deeper buy?

Depth 5: the wanted articles reach the merge 94% of the time, 94% end up in the top 10, 28.6 ms per query. Depth 20: the wanted articles reach the merge 100% of the time, 99% end up in the top 10, 76.4 ms per query. Depth 100: the wanted articles reach the merge 100% of the time, 99% end up in the top 10, 230 ms per query. Merging and reranking can only reorder what the lanes handed over: an article that one lane ranked 8th and the other missed is gone at depth 5. Once the right articles are reliably in the pool, more depth only buys reranking time. Measure where that point is for your own queries.

5. A reranker over the top 100

Experiment 5 of 5
RRF over each lane's top 100 · then a cross-encoder reads the query and each of the top 100 together and rescores them (2.2 ms a candidate) · is the wanted article in the top 5?
What does the reranker change?

Without: exact IDs 87%, paraphrases 78%, mixed 96% in the top 5, 10 ms. With: exact IDs 100%, paraphrases 87%, mixed 96%, 230 ms. The two lanes score the query and an article separately, from a word count or two vectors; a cross-encoder reads them side by side, so it sees the product name, the identifier and the meaning at once. It is still a model and still makes mistakes, and it is too slow to run over a whole corpus, so it reorders a shortlist: it can't find what the lanes missed, and its cost grows with how many candidates it reads.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1A customer pastes "E-4072" into search, another types "my money back for a cracked parcel". Why does one retriever handle the first and miss the second, and the other the opposite?
A good answer coversBM25 scores exact tokens, weighted by how rare they are: an identifier is rare, so the one article that contains it wins. But it has no idea that money back means refund or cracked means damaged. An embedding model puts words with similar meaning close together, so the paraphrase lands near the refund articles, but an identifier or a product name carries little learned meaning, so every error-code article looks about the same to it.
Q2You merge BM25 and vector results with a weighted sum and α seems to do nothing. What is probably wrong?
A good answer coversThe scores are on different scales: BM25 runs from 0 to tens, cosine from -1 to 1. Unscaled, the bigger numbers decide and α barely matters. Normalise each list first (min-max to 0..1, or z-scores), or use reciprocal rank fusion, which only looks at ranks: 1 / (60 + rank) from each list, summed.
Q3How would you choose α, or decide between RRF and a weighted sum?
A good answer coversBuild a labelled set of real queries and the articles that should come back, broken down by kind, and measure recall@k or nDCG for each setting. RRF is a good default with nothing to tune. A weighted sum can beat it once tuned, but its best α depends on your query mix, so retune when the mix changes; some systems pick α per query, from how many identifiers the query contains.
Q4With plain RRF, hybrid scored a little below the meaning lane alone on paraphrase queries. Why can merging cost a few points on one kind of query, and what can you do?
A good answer coversFusion gives seats in the top k to whatever either list ranks well. On a paraphrase the keyword lane is the weaker one, and its top results include near-misses that share a word or two; plain RRF gives them the same vote as the meaning lane's results. Weight the lists (weighted RRF, or a tuned α), cap the weaker list's depth, or let a reranker reorder the merged list. Overall, hybrid still comes out ahead because it fixes the other lane's much bigger misses.
Q5Why rerank only the top 100 or so, and why can't a reranker fix a bad retrieval stage?
A good answer coversA cross-encoder reads the query and each candidate together, which is accurate but costs a model call per candidate, far too slow for every document. So retrieval is the recall stage (get the right documents into the shortlist cheaply) and reranking the precision stage (put them in the right order). Anything retrieval left out never reaches the reranker, so depth per lane sets the ceiling.
Q6Where does this show up in real systems?
A good answer coversRetrieval for RAG and site search: Elasticsearch and OpenSearch can combine a BM25 query with a kNN query and merge by RRF or normalised scores; Weaviate's hybrid search has an alpha (1 = pure vector); Postgres can pair full-text search with pgvector and merge in SQL. The design questions are the same as here: which lanes, how to merge, how deep, and whether a reranker is worth its latency.
