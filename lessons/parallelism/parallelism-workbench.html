Systems lesson 14 · Parallelism

Parallelism Workbench

Parallelism is doing many pieces of work at the same instant, on several cores or machines. More workers help only as far as the work can be split, shared things allow and coordination costs. This lesson builds that idea three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from the same model as the machine, averaged over six seeded runs of one job.

1. Eight workers

Experiment 1 of 5
1 → 8 workers on 8 benches · 10% of the job is packing, which only one station can do
How much faster does a job finish?

Only the parallel part gets eight times faster. The 10% at the packing station still takes as long as before, so it soon dominates: Amdahl's law caps 10% serial work on 8 cores at 4.7×, and handing out and merging chunks costs a little more. No number of workers can beat 10× here, because the serial part alone takes a tenth of the original time.

2. More workers than benches

Experiment 2 of 5
8 → 16 workers · still 8 benches (cores) · job split into 16 chunks
What happens to the job time?

A bench can only work on one thing at an instant. Two workers on one bench take turns, and every switch costs a little (on a CPU: saving and restoring state, cold caches). More threads than cores helps only when threads spend time waiting on something other than the CPU; for CPU-bound work, match the worker count to the cores.

3. One shared tool

Experiment 3 of 5
Shared tool off → on · the last 40% of every chunk needs the one tool on the rack
With 8 workers, how fast is the job compared with one worker?

The tool is a lock: only one worker can hold it, so the part of each chunk that needs it is serial again, and workers queue for it. Every shared, exclusive resource (a lock, a single database row, one connection) adds to the serial share in Amdahl's law. Speed-up comes from removing what workers share, not from adding workers.

4. A tiny job

Experiment 4 of 5
A job of 0.3 s instead of 16 s · 1 worker with 1 chunk vs 8 workers with 8 chunks
Which finishes first?

Splitting has a fixed cost per chunk: the foreman hands each one out and the packing station merges each one back. On a big job that cost is lost in the noise; on a tiny one it is bigger than the work saved. Parallelise work that is large compared with the cost of coordinating it, and batch small items together.

5. One heavy chunk

Experiment 5 of 5
Uneven data: the first eighth of the job holds a third of the work · 8 chunks vs 32 chunks, 8 workers
With 8 chunks, what happens to the job time?

The job only finishes when its slowest chunk does. With 8 chunks, one worker gets the heavy one while seven finish early and stand idle. Cutting the job into 32 smaller chunks lets free workers keep taking the next one, which spreads the heavy part out. That is what work-stealing schedulers and dynamic batching do.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1Your batch job takes 60 minutes on one core and 20% of it can't be parallelised. What's the best it can do on 16 cores, and on infinitely many?
A good answer coversAmdahl: 1 / (0.2 + 0.8/16) = 4×, so 15 minutes. With infinite cores it approaches 1/0.2 = 5×, 12 minutes: the serial part alone takes 12 minutes. To go further you have to shrink the serial part, not add cores.
Q2What's the difference between concurrency and parallelism?
A good answer coversConcurrency is dealing with many things at once: tasks overlap in time, even on one core, by taking turns. Parallelism is doing many things at the same instant, which needs several cores or machines. Concurrency is about structure; parallelism is about execution.
Q3A teammate sets a thread pool to 200 threads on an 8-core server to make CPU-heavy image resizing faster. What do you expect?
A good answer coversNo speed-up beyond 8 at a time, plus extra switching and memory per thread, so it may get slower. For CPU-bound work, size the pool near the core count; large pools only help work that mostly waits on I/O.
Q4Why can adding workers make a program slower?
A good answer coversCoordination overhead (splitting, scheduling, merging) and contention on shared things: a lock, a shared counter, a single connection, cache lines bouncing between cores. If those grow with the number of workers, past some point each new worker costs more than it adds.
Q5Your parallel job's progress bar sits at 95% for a long time. What's the likely cause and the fix?
A good answer coversUneven work: one chunk is much bigger than the others, so one worker is still busy while the rest are idle. Use smaller chunks with a shared queue (or a work-stealing scheduler), or split by work done rather than by item count.
Q6Where do you meet these ideas outside threads?
A good answer coversEverywhere work is spread out: map-reduce and Spark jobs, CI pipelines split across runners, database queries using parallel workers, Kubernetes jobs with many pods. The same rules hold: the serial step (a final merge, a single writer), coordination cost and stragglers decide the speed-up.
