Systems lesson 39 · Contextual retrieval

Contextual Retrieval Workbench

Cut a document into chunks and most chunks forget whose document they came from: "the company's revenue grew by 3%" could be any company, any quarter. Contextual Retrieval has an LLM read the whole document and write a short context for each chunk, prepended before the chunk is embedded and indexed for keywords. This lesson runs it as a print shop: watch a machine do it, predict what each step buys, then explain it back.

01

The machine

Reports are cut into chunk cards; a press that reads the whole report stamps each card with its context; the cards are filed by meaning and by keyword. Each question pulls candidates into the rack, an inspector reranks them, and the tray is what the model gets. Switch the context, the keyword index, the reranker and the number passed on, and watch the misses. The technique and the published numbers are from Anthropic's Introducing Contextual Retrieval (September 2024).

The corpus is synthetic: 128 quarterly filings from 16 companies over two years and 8 product manuals, cut into 816 chunks. 02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results come from this lesson's model (real BM25, a toy embedding, a simple reranker) over six seeded corpora; the post's measured numbers on real data are in each explanation.

1. Stamp the context on every card

Experiment 1 of 5
Search by meaning only (embeddings) · plain chunk cards → cards with the context header stamped on before they are embedded · top 20 passed on
What happens to the share of questions whose answer chunk isn't among the 20 passed on?

A plain chunk like "The company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter." reads the same in every filing that reports revenue (about 64 here), so a question about Acme's second quarter matches all of them about equally; a chunk about a recall or a lawsuit is rarer, so it is usually found anyway. The stamped header names the company and quarter, and the embedding now leans toward the right chunk. Only leans: embeddings carry names weakly (to an embedding one company looks a lot like another) and pay little attention to exact codes, so some questions still miss. The post measured the same direction on real data: Contextual Embeddings cut the top-20 failure rate by 35%, from 5.7% to 3.7%.

2. Search the words as well

Experiment 2 of 5
Cards with context · meaning only → meaning plus a BM25 keyword index of the same stamped cards, the two rankings merged by reciprocal rank fusion
Adding a keyword index to the meaning search: what happens to the failures?

BM25 rewards exact, rare words: "Acme", "2023", a product name. With the context stamped on, every Acme chunk now carries "Acme", so the keyword index can pick Acme's chunk out of the look-alikes, which the embedding could only lean toward. It can't help when the question says "ACM" or "the second quarter" and the header says "Acme Corp" and "Q2": exact words only. The meaning search still covers paraphrases ("sales rise" for "revenue grew"), and rank fusion (here weighted 0.8 meaning, 0.2 keywords) keeps what either list puts near the top. On plain chunks the company name appears only on the cover, so BM25 has nothing to match and changes almost nothing. The post: Contextual Embeddings plus Contextual BM25 cut the failure rate by 49% (5.7% → 2.9%).

3. Rerank 150, keep 20

Experiment 3 of 5
Context, meaning and BM25 · pass the top 20 straight on → fetch the top 150, let a reranker that reads question and chunk together reorder them, pass its top 20 on
What does the reranker change?

First-stage search is fast and rough; a reranker is slower but reads the question and the whole chunk (with its context) together, so it can put the right chunk first even when it came back 60th. It can only reorder what it is given, though: an answer outside the 150 stays missed, and on plain chunks it has nothing to tell the look-alikes apart with, so it just reshuffles them. The post retrieved 150, reranked with Cohere's reranker and kept 20: failures fell by 67% overall (5.7% → 1.9%). The cost is a little latency and money per query.

4. How many chunks to pass on

Experiment 4 of 5
The full pipeline (context, meaning + BM25, reranked) · pass on the top 5 / 10 / 20 chunks
Which passes the answer to the model most often?

Even after reranking, the right chunk is sometimes 6th or 12th, so a short list drops it. Twenty chunks of 800 tokens is still a small prompt for a modern model. The post tried 5, 10 and 20 and found 20 worked best. Past that, more chunks cost tokens and can bury the answer among near-misses, so measure on your own questions.

5. What the context costs, and when to skip all this

Experiment 5 of 5
Writing a 100-token context for every 800-token chunk of 8k-token documents, with Claude 3 Haiku at list prices · without vs with prompt caching of the document
Roughly what does contextualising a million tokens of documents cost with prompt caching, and when don't you need retrieval at all?

Each chunk's call sends the whole document plus the chunk. Without caching you pay for the full document again for every chunk; with caching the document is written to the cache once and read cheaply for the rest. The post estimates $1.02 per million document tokens with caching, a one-time cost at indexing. If the whole knowledge base is under about 200,000 tokens (about 500 pages), the post's advice is to skip retrieval and put all of it in the prompt, with prompt caching to keep that cheap. This lesson's corpus stands for well over 200,000 tokens; one company's filings would fit.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1Why does chunking lose information, and what exactly does Contextual Retrieval add?
A good answer coversA chunk is cut out of its document, so whatever the document said once, up front (which company, which quarter, which product), is missing from most chunks. Contextual Retrieval asks an LLM, given the whole document and one chunk, for a short chunk-specific context (usually 50 to 100 tokens), and prepends it to the chunk before the chunk is embedded and before it is added to the BM25 index. The chunk shown to the model at answer time can be the same text.
Q2Why do embeddings alone struggle with "Acme's revenue in Q2 2023", and why does BM25 help?
A good answer coversEmbeddings capture meaning, which is great for paraphrases, but they blur exact identifiers: one company name, quarter or error code looks much like another. BM25 scores exact words, weighting rare ones heavily, so it pins down "Acme" or "TS-999". Once the context has put those names into every chunk, the keyword index can use them. Combining both, with rank fusion, gets meaning and exact matches.
Q3What is reranking, and why retrieve 150 to keep 20?
A good answer coversThe first stage scores the query against every chunk separately (precomputed vectors, an inverted index), which is fast but rough. A reranker reads the query and each candidate together, which is more accurate but far too slow to run on everything. So you retrieve generously (150) for recall, rerank, and keep the best 20 for the prompt. It can only reorder candidates: whatever the first stage missed stays missed.
Q4How does prompt caching make contextualisation cheap?
A good answer coversEvery chunk of a document needs a call that includes the whole document. Cache the document after the first chunk's call, and the other calls read it from the cache at a fraction of the input price. The post estimates about $1.02 per million document tokens, paid once when you index.
Q5When would you not build any of this?
A good answer coversWhen the knowledge base is small: under about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages) you can put all of it in the prompt and use prompt caching, with no chunking, no index and no retrieval failures. Retrieval earns its complexity when the corpus is too big for the context window or too costly to resend.
Q6How would you measure whether Contextual Retrieval helps on your own data?
A good answer coversBuild a set of real questions with the chunk (or chunks) that answer each. Measure the failure rate at your top-K: the share of questions whose answer isn't among the chunks you pass on. Change one thing at a time (context, BM25, reranking, K, the embedding model, the context prompt) and compare on the same set. Look at the misses: they tell you whether the problem is ambiguity, wording or ranking.
