Systems lesson 04 · Circuit breakers

Circuit Breaker Workbench

When a service you depend on gets slow or fails, the danger is not the failed calls: it is your own workers waiting on them. This lesson shows how timeouts, retries and a circuit breaker change that, three ways: watch a machine do it, predict what breaks it, then explain it back.

02

Predict, then run

Each experiment changes one thing. Choose what you expect before running it. Results are averaged over eight seeded runs of the same machine, about 50 to 70 seconds of traffic each.

1. The supplier slows down

Experiment 1 of 5
Supplier healthy → slow · each call now takes about 6 s · no timeout, no breaker
Three in ten requests never call the supplier. What happens to them?

Every worker that picks up a supplier request sits on the line for 6 seconds. Requests needing the supplier arrive faster than that, so soon all six workers are stuck waiting and the tray at the door is full. Requests that would take 0.4 s on their own are turned away because there is nobody to serve them. A slow dependency is worse than a dead one: a dead one at least answers quickly.

2. Add a timeout

Experiment 2 of 5
Slow supplier, as before · timeout off → 1.5 s
With a 1.5 s timeout, what happens to the requests that need the supplier?

A timeout doesn't make the supplier faster. It caps how long a worker can be held, so workers come back and the requests that don't need the supplier are served again. The supplier requests still fail, and every one of them waits the full timeout first.

3. Add the breaker

Experiment 3 of 5
Supplier failing · timeout 1.5 s · breaker off → on (opens after 5 failures in a row, 5 s cooldown)
With the breaker on, what happens to the load on the supplier?

After five failures in a row, the breaker opens and requests fail at the breaker instead of waiting on the line. Your workers are free, and the supplier stops receiving calls, apart from one probe after each cooldown. The requests still fail, but quickly, and the supplier gets room to recover.

4. Retry three times

Experiment 4 of 5
Supplier failing, fixed after 20 s · timeout 1.5 s · retries 0 → 3, no backoff, no breaker
The cause is fixed after 20 s. With three immediate retries per request, when do supplier calls succeed again?

Each failing request now makes up to four calls, and abandoned calls keep running on the supplier. Its load climbs past what it can handle, so it stays overloaded after the original cause is fixed. The retries become the outage. A breaker in front of the retries cuts the load and lets it recover.

5. A long cooldown

Experiment 5 of 5
Supplier failing, fixed after 20 s · timeout 1.5 s · breaker on · cooldown 5 s → 30 s
With a 30 s cooldown, how soon after the fix does traffic flow again?

An open breaker doesn't watch the supplier. It waits out the cooldown, then lets one probe through (half-open). If the probe succeeds the breaker closes; if it fails it opens for another cooldown. A long cooldown keeps you failing after the supplier is healthy; a short one probes more often but costs little, since it's one call.

03

Explain it back

Answer out loud or in writing before opening each one. These work well as a pairing exercise: one person explains, the other asks "why?" twice.

Q1Why can a slow dependency hurt you more than one that is down?
A good answer coversA dead dependency fails fast, so your workers are released at once. A slow one holds each worker for the whole call. Once every worker is waiting, nothing else can be served, not even requests that never touch that dependency. That is how one slow service takes down endpoints that have nothing to do with it.
Q2How would you choose a timeout for a call to another service?
A good answer coversStart from that service's normal latency (for example its p99 plus a margin) and keep it shorter than your own caller's deadline. Too long and workers pile up behind a slow call; too short and normal jitter turns into false failures. A timeout should be set on purpose, never left at a library default of 30 s or infinity.
Q3A circuit breaker protects whom: your service, the supplier, or both?
A good answer coversBoth. It releases your workers by failing fast, and it takes load off the supplier so it can recover. The cost is that requests fail without even trying while it is open, so you need a fallback for them.
Q4Why can automatic retries make an outage longer, and how do you retry safely?
A good answer coversRetries multiply the load at the worst moment: three retries can mean four times the calls to a struggling service, which keeps it overloaded after the original cause is fixed. Retry only idempotent operations, keep the count small, add exponential backoff with jitter, use a retry budget, and put retries behind a breaker.
Q5What is the half-open state for, and what goes wrong if the cooldown is too long or too short?
A good answer coversAfter the cooldown, half-open lets a single probe through to test the supplier without sending it full traffic. Success closes the breaker; failure opens it again. Too long a cooldown keeps you failing after the supplier has recovered; too short a cooldown makes it flap, which costs little load but makes noisy alerts and logs.
Q6The breaker is open. What should the user see?
A good answer coversA fast, honest response: a cached or default value, a degraded feature ("recommendations unavailable"), or a clear error. Not a spinner that waits for a timeout. Deciding the fallback is part of adding a breaker.
